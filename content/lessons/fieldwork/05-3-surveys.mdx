<Callout variant="key" title="What a survey adds to the mixed-methods package">
A survey collects data from a sample of the broader population targeted by the evaluation. When the sample is randomly selected, findings can be generalised — within statistical bounds — to the population the respondents are drawn from. In a CPE, surveys are particularly suited to assessing **rights-holders' satisfaction** with specific UNFPA-supported services.
</Callout>

<h3>Building the questionnaire</h3>

A survey questionnaire usually combines two question types:

<KVGrid>
  <KV title="Structured (closed) questions">Respondents choose from a predetermined set of responses. Aim: facts, agreement or disagreement, opinions on assertions. Allow statistical analysis — counts, percentages, distributions.</KV>

  <KV title="A few open-ended questions">Allow variety and depth in response. Yield richer, more precise information — but less amenable to statistical analysis. Typical openers: "why", "how", "what", "describe", "tell me about", "what do you think about".</KV>
</KVGrid>

<h3>Administration modes</h3>

Surveys can be administered in many ways: **in-person on paper** (e.g. during site visits), via mail, by web (SurveyMonkey, KoboCollect), by phone, or — for hard-to-reach populations — by SMS. Whatever the mode, the team has to think from the respondent's perspective about what will maximise participation:

<KVGrid>
  <KV title="Accessibility">Can the intended respondent actually receive and complete this survey? SMS may reach where the web cannot.</KV>

  <KV title="Format convenience">Time to complete; design clarity; question flow; ease of response.</KV>

  <KV title="Purpose clarity">The cover note tells the respondent why this matters, who's asking, and what happens with their answers.</KV>

  <KV title="Wording and length">Long surveys lose respondents. Cut to what the matrix actually needs verified.</KV>
</KVGrid>

<h3>Analysing the data</h3>

Once survey data is in, analysis looks for **relationships, patterns, and trends**. It may involve comparison with data from other groups. When both quantitative (closed questions) and qualitative (open-ended) survey data are analysed together, the team can:

- **Quantify and compare the importance of diverse opinions** — count how many respondents share a view.
- **Check whether the population agrees or not** with positions surfaced in interviews, and in what proportions.
- **Analyse rights-holders' degree of satisfaction** with specific UNFPA-supported services.

<Callout variant="warn" title="The sample-size guarantee">
The evaluation team must verify that **the sample of surveyed informants is large enough and representative** of the targeted population. A small, biased sample wrapped in survey rigour is more misleading than a few honest interviews — because the numbers create false confidence. Sample size, response rate, and any non-response bias must be documented in the matrix and the eventual report.
</Callout>

<h3>Where surveys fit (and where they don't)</h3>

A well-prepared survey is **cost-effective and time-efficient**, freeing evaluators to dedicate time to other more time-consuming tasks. But Table 10 (lesson 2.2.3) flags real limitations:

<KVGrid>
  <KV title="Survey strengths">Affordable; yields quantified and reliable data; useful to identify changes and make comparisons; reaches a large number of persons; respondents may be more honest if the survey is not administered in person; highly accessible/convenient for many respondents.</KV>

  <KV title="Survey limitations">Implementation time can exceed the CPE data-collection window; analysis takes time; representative sampling is difficult; high abandonment rates if the questionnaire is unfriendly; some vulnerable groups may not have easy access; in humanitarian settings (displacement, hard-to-reach areas, insecurity) extensive surveys may not be feasible.</KV>
</KVGrid>

<InPractice country="A practical pattern">
Many CPEs use surveys for narrow, high-volume questions — satisfaction with a specific service, awareness of a campaign, take-up of a commodity — and rely on KIIs and FGDs for the strategic, contextual, and unintended-effect material. The matrix should be explicit about which evaluation question each method serves; otherwise the team risks doing a small, low-power survey instead of doing what the question actually needed.
</InPractice>

<KeyTerms>
  <Term name="Structured question">A closed question with a fixed response set — the workhorse of quantitative survey analysis.</Term>
  <Term name="Open-ended question">A free-text question yielding qualitative content — used sparingly in surveys because it doesn't aggregate cleanly.</Term>
  <Term name="Representative sample">A sample drawn so that its characteristics match the targeted population — required for findings to be generalisable.</Term>
  <Term name="Response rate">The proportion of invited respondents who actually completed the survey. Low response rates can indicate non-response bias.</Term>
</KeyTerms>
