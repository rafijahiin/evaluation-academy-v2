<Callout variant="key" title="Why photographs matter">
Photographs from interviews, group discussions, and site visits enhance evaluation reporting and support effective communication of evaluation results. They add a human face to UNFPA interventions and make the evaluation's key messages more memorable — facilitating the **use** of the evaluation results in dissemination.
</Callout>

<h3>Two kinds of photographs</h3>

<KVGrid>
  <KV title="Process pictures">Show the evaluation in action — focus groups in progress, interviews with officials, presentations to CO staff. Used in social media and presentations. Evaluation team members may appear in these.</KV>

  <KV title="Programme pictures">Show UNFPA's work, not the team. Rights-holders in their setting (individuals or groups); services and supplies (kits, tents, equipment) supported by UNFPA. Used in the evaluation report and dissemination products. No evaluation team member visible.</KV>
</KVGrid>

<h3>Consent — non-negotiable</h3>

<Callout variant="warn" title="Three consent rules">
**Rule 1.** People who can be identified in a photograph must give permission to be photographed and for the use of their image in multimedia formats — written reports, videos, presentations, websites, social media — including the use of their names in captions.

**Rule 2.** For children under 18, parental or guardian consent must be sought.

**Rule 3.** To protect the identity of individuals who may be put at risk by being photographed, make sure they cannot be identified in the picture — and leave personal information out of the metadata.

The team uses the **UNFPA multimedia consent form** before taking photographs.
</Callout>

<h3>Recording and handover</h3>

After taking a photograph, the team records the date, site, and subject — plus a brief caption. All pictures are shared with the CPE Manager and the CO communication officer through a shared drive.

Photographs taken by the evaluation team during data collection are **credited to UNFPA**. The CPE Manager ensures the CO communication officer reviews the photographic material and attends the debriefing meeting (lesson 3.4.2).

<Checklist storageKey="ch3-l7-photography" title="Photography readiness checklist">
  <Item>The CO communication officer briefs the team on ethical photography during data collection.</Item>
  <Item>UNFPA multimedia consent form is printed in sufficient copies.</Item>
  <Item>Parental/guardian consent process is in place for any photograph involving a person under 18.</Item>
  <Item>Identifying features of at-risk individuals are obscured in the image or by framing.</Item>
  <Item>Each image has its date, site, subject, and caption recorded immediately.</Item>
  <Item>Photographs are uploaded to the shared drive accessible to the CPE Manager and CO communication officer.</Item>
  <Item>Before the report goes out, the CPE Manager and CO communication officer revisit the initial communication plan and adjust based on the photographic material collected.</Item>
</Checklist>

<InPractice country="Photography as dissemination capital">
Many CPEs treat photography as an afterthought. The handbook frames it as part of dissemination strategy — the visuals taken in week 2 of fieldwork are what carry the findings into ministers' briefings, partner workshops, and UNFPA's public reporting in week 40. The investment in consent and quality during data collection pays for itself across the entire dissemination phase.
</InPractice>

<KeyTerms>
  <Term name="Process picture">A photograph of the evaluation in action (FGD in progress, interview underway) — evaluation team members may appear.</Term>
  <Term name="Programme picture">A photograph of UNFPA's work — rights-holders, services, commodities — with no evaluation team member visible. Used in the report and dissemination products.</Term>
  <Term name="Multimedia consent form">The UNFPA-issued consent document signed by people being photographed (with parental consent additionally where the subject is under 18).</Term>
  <Term name="Identifiability">Whether a person can be recognised in the image. For at-risk individuals, identifiability must be eliminated by framing or post-processing.</Term>
</KeyTerms>
