<Callout variant="key" title="What an interview gives you that a document can't">
Interviews surface stakeholders' **perceptions** of UNFPA interventions — whether they agree with analyses in official reports, and where their views diverge. They reveal **conflicting guidance** (UNFPA vs partner agencies) and **unintended results** that monitoring data tends to miss. They are predominantly a qualitative tool — facts, opinions, analyses, and reactions to the evaluators' hypotheses must be clearly distinguished when entering data into the matrix.
</Callout>

<h3>What evaluators must distinguish in their notes</h3>

<KVGrid>
  <KV title="Facts (verifiable)">Statements that can be checked against documents, datasets, or other interviewees. Date of policy adoption, number of midwives trained, year a clinic opened.</KV>

  <KV title="Points of view / opinions">The interviewee's interpretation of events or their preferred direction. Useful — but tagged as opinion, not fact.</KV>

  <KV title="Analyses">The interviewee's reasoning about cause-and-effect or about what shaped outcomes. Often the most valuable interview content for the contribution analysis.</KV>

  <KV title="Reactions to the evaluators' hypotheses">When evaluators probe assumptions from the matrix and capture how each interviewee responds — agree, partially agree, disagree, refine.</KV>
</KVGrid>

<h3>Sample sizes and timing</h3>

The interview is also a tool with **representativeness limits**, particularly when speaking with rights-holders and the most vulnerable. Information collected must be systematically checked through other data collection tools and other sources — same question asked across different institutional viewpoints (government, implementing partners, rights-holders, CSOs, development partners).

The team uses the **interview guides developed in the design phase** to conduct **semi-structured interviews**. The guides provide a framework that is not binding on the evaluator — they can adjust based on informant, context, time, and other factors. Typical durations:

<KVGrid>
  <KV title="Individual KII">30 to 40 minutes on average; longer where the range of topics or the informant's depth warrants it.</KV>

  <KV title="Group interview">Up to 1h30 maximum — including time for everyone to be heard.</KV>

  <KV title="Focus group discussion">Up to 1h30 maximum — especially with rights-holders, whose time often comes from income-earning hours.</KV>
</KVGrid>

<h3>Box 12 — Interview sequencing</h3>

<Checklist storageKey="ch3-l3-interview-sequence" title="Box 12 — How to run a key informant interview">
  <Item>Make a human connection — friendly remarks before starting.</Item>
  <Item>Thank the interviewee for their time; introduce yourself; state the purpose (the evaluation of the UNFPA country programme to inform the way forward — not an assessment of the interviewee or their organisation).</Item>
  <Item>Assure complete confidentiality; stress no information will be attributable to individuals; assure they need not answer questions they're uncomfortable with — particularly important with rights-holders and young people.</Item>
  <Item>Briefly indicate the main areas of focus (per the guide), and check whether the interviewee has any questions before starting.</Item>
  <Item>Check the nature of the interviewee's role in relation to the UNFPA country programme and how long they've been involved.</Item>
  <Item>Go through each core issue, framing them as questions appropriate for this interviewee. Feel like a focused conversation — do not express personal opinions; do not criticise the interviewee's. Seek clarification by reframing or reflecting back, especially where contradictions surface.</Item>
  <Item>If using SWOT: include it early to surface probing areas, or late to corroborate what the interviewee finds most important.</Item>
  <Item>Before closing, ask if there is anything important or relevant not yet addressed.</Item>
  <Item>Invite questions from the interviewee — often about when the report will be ready, whether they'll see it, dissemination meetings. Capture their interest in being invited to dissemination.</Item>
  <Item>Reassure on confidentiality; thank again; ask if there are other stakeholders the team should meet; offer to follow up by email if more information is needed.</Item>
</Checklist>

<h3>What evaluators should and should not do</h3>

<KVGrid>
  <KV title="Do — avoid jargon">Particularly with rights-holders. Translate UN/UNFPA terms into language the interviewee uses.</KV>

  <KV title="Do — cross-check the complex">Probe "why" and "how" as well as "what" and "when".</KV>

  <KV title="Don't — ask leading questions">Don't push interviewees toward the answer you think you should hear.</KV>

  <KV title="Don't — judge opinions">Explore the basis of an opinion; don't react to its content.</KV>

  <KV title="Do — be open about gaps">Ask follow-up questions when something isn't clear. Acknowledging what you don't know builds the conversation.</KV>
</KVGrid>

<h3>Virtual interviews — when and how</h3>

<Callout variant="tip" title="Field travel isn't always feasible">
If travel is impracticable — distance, cost, security, health risks, poor data availability — greater reliance falls on centrally available informants and extensive documentary review. During COVID-19, virtually all data collection had to be remote. A typical CPE today involves a **mix of face-to-face and virtual interviews**. Virtual interviews lower the cost of reaching more stakeholders, but they restrict participation by vulnerable groups without good connectivity. If an interview cannot be fully completed (time, connection), follow up with a brief supplementary questionnaire by email.
</Callout>

<h3>Box 13 — Reliability of data: three biases and their controls</h3>

<KVGrid>
  <KV title="Self-censorship">**Risk:** informants are reluctant to answer freely and instead voice their institution's line. **Control:** the evaluators guarantee confidentiality and anonymity in how answers are treated — and they keep that promise strictly.</KV>

  <KV title="Informants' strategy">**Risk:** informants distort information to push the evaluation toward conclusions they prefer. **Control:** the CPE Manager and team leader never rely on a single category of stakeholders; the sample covers a wide range; triangulation is thorough.</KV>

  <KV title="Question-induced answers">**Risk:** evaluators ask in ways that pre-empt the answer (leading questions, reacting to responses, creating an atmosphere that pulls a particular answer). **Control:** mix positive and negative questions; test questionnaires in advance.</KV>
</KVGrid>

<InPractice country="The interviewee's question back to you">
A common closing moment: the interviewee asks when the report will be ready, whether they'll see it, whether they'll be invited to a dissemination event. The handbook expects the team to capture these requests — and the CPE Manager will use the list to plan the dissemination phase (lesson 5.2). What you tell each interviewee at the end is also what shapes whether they trust the process enough to talk to the next evaluation.
</InPractice>

<KeyTerms>
  <Term name="Semi-structured interview">An interview using a guide as a framework, with the evaluator free to adjust order, depth, and follow-up based on the conversation.</Term>
  <Term name="Recall bias">Risk that participants struggle to accurately remember past events — flagged in Table 10 as a KII limitation.</Term>
  <Term name="Representativeness">The extent to which interviewed informants reflect the population whose experience is being studied. Always a KII limitation, requiring triangulation.</Term>
  <Term name="Semi-structured guide">The cluster-specific document developed in the design phase that frames every KII.</Term>
</KeyTerms>
