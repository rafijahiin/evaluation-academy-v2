<Callout variant="key" title="What observation captures that nothing else does">
On-site observation gathers data by watching **people's interactions, processes, or behaviours as they occur in their natural setting**. It's direct (a service provider's interaction with a patient) or indirect (the state of equipment, stockouts, accessibility features). Site visits let the team see "what is actually happening" at locations where UNFPA interventions are implemented — and increase their understanding of how interventions work in practice.
</Callout>

<h3>Refining the design-phase plan</h3>

At the design phase, the team planned observation activities (lesson 2.2.3). Now they refine them based on what the fieldwork agenda allows. Two decisions matter:

<KVGrid>
  <KV title="Focus">Which evaluation questions or assumptions can usefully be informed by observation? Not everything in the matrix benefits — but some assumptions only get a fair test from on-site evidence.</KV>

  <KV title="Specific areas of attention">Concrete things to look at. Example: if assessing whether training of health providers has changed service quality, the focus could be: service-provider–rights-holder interactions; applied knowledge; new skills in use; changed behaviours.</KV>
</KVGrid>

The team needs to be clear about **which observed aspects relate to UNFPA contributions and which don't** — many features at a site exist independently of UNFPA support, and good evaluators don't credit (or blame) UNFPA for things UNFPA didn't shape.

<h3>The observation checklist</h3>

Observation is conducted using the **on-site observation checklist** prepared at design. The checklist makes data comparable across sites. Examples of what evaluators look at:

<KVGrid>
  <KV title="Privacy">For patients in a maternity unit; for HIV testing and counselling at sites where UNFPA supported staff training.</KV>

  <KV title="Physical state of materials and equipment">Training materials and equipment UNFPA helped develop or procure — dilapidated or unused equipment has direct implications for the effectiveness of UNFPA support.</KV>

  <KV title="Actual use and maintenance">Beyond presence — is the equipment being used? Maintained? In service?</KV>

  <KV title="Commodities and supplies">Availability and storage of maternal and reproductive health commodities; cold-chain conditions where relevant; stockouts.</KV>

  <KV title="Accessibility">For people with disabilities; the extent to which the site is youth-friendly; signage; physical access.</KV>

  <KV title="Operations">Availability of staff; opening hours; waiting times; throughput.</KV>
</KVGrid>

<h3>Site visits as team work</h3>

<Callout variant="tip" title="More than one evaluator per site">
Site visits should be conducted by **more than one evaluator** — or by all members of the team — to gain different perspectives and divide tasks. At a health centre: one evaluator gathers administrative data (patient flow, record handling), others conduct brief interviews with service providers, and the team runs a patient exit survey or a focus group to obtain rights-holders' views.
</Callout>

Mixing methods and roles within the team during the same visit gives the team **multiple perspectives on the same site** — and lets the evaluation matrix populate across several methods in one trip.

<h3>Timing and "best-behaviour" risk</h3>

Activities at a site usually follow a sequence — specific days/hours for family-planning consultations, antenatal clinics, particular staff rosters. The CPE Manager helps the team **time observation appropriately** so they see the activity they came for.

<Callout variant="warn" title="The 'best behaviour' problem">
Short site visits face two limits: (1) the team may not be able to grasp the complexity or dynamics of the situation; (2) people may "put on a good face" knowing they're being observed. In some contexts, the way adolescents or key populations are served differs on the day evaluators arrive. **Careful observation, complemented by skillful interviewing and well-prepared surveys**, is how the team counteracts false impressions.
</Callout>

<h3>What Table 10 says about observation</h3>

<KVGrid>
  <KV title="Strengths (Table 10)">Captures data where and when an event or activity is occurring. Does not rely on people's willingness or ability to provide information. Lets evaluators directly see what people do — rather than what they say they do.</KV>

  <KV title="Limitations (Table 10)">People perform better when they know they are being observed (the Hawthorne effect). Observation can be time-consuming compared to other methods. Observation alone doesn't explain WHY people behave as they do — interviews are needed for that.</KV>
</KVGrid>

<InPractice country="A real site-visit pattern">
At a UNFPA-supported maternity unit: one team member shadows the labour ward; another examines the supply room and reviews the stockout register; a third runs a 20-minute patient exit interview with women leaving the unit. The same site, three methods, two hours. Each method's data feeds a different row of the evaluation matrix — and the contradictions among them often become the most interesting finding.
</InPractice>

<KeyTerms>
  <Term name="Direct observation">Watching people's actions and interactions as they happen — service delivery, training, meetings.</Term>
  <Term name="Indirect observation">Inferring what's happening from artefacts and conditions — equipment state, stockout records, accessibility features.</Term>
  <Term name="Observation checklist">The standard list of focus areas prepared at design, used identically across sites for comparability.</Term>
  <Term name="Hawthorne effect">The performance change that occurs because subjects know they're being observed — managed by extended visits, multiple methods, and triangulation.</Term>
</KeyTerms>
