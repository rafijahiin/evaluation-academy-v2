<Callout variant="key" title="Group interview vs focus group — they are not the same">
A **group interview** gathers many opinions in a group setting, but is largely structured as an interviewer–each-individual exchange. A **focus group discussion** is interactive — the group's collective view is at least as important as any one person's. The moderator's job is to guide the interaction so the collective perspective on an issue surfaces.
</Callout>

<h3>When to use a focus group</h3>

Focus groups give evaluators access to **socially homogeneous categories of stakeholders** and let them understand the reasoning behind shared opinions. They are particularly useful for:

<KVGrid>
  <KV title="Rights-holders in communities">Mothers; adolescent girls; small groups of vulnerable or marginalised populations (women with disabilities, LGBTQIA+ youth, etc.); UNFPA-supported beneficiaries.</KV>

  <KV title="Health-service providers at sites">Doctors, nurses, midwives, community health workers — discussing service delivery experience first-hand.</KV>

  <KV title="CSOs collectively">A small representative group from different CSOs reflecting on the needs orientation of UNFPA support and continued benefits after interventions end.</KV>

  <KV title="UNFPA staff and partner staff">Where the question is about collective implementation experience rather than individual perspectives.</KV>
</KVGrid>

<h3>Setting up a focus group</h3>

The team identifies interest groups among the stakeholder categories concerned by the assessed interventions. With UNFPA staff (or implementing partners) helping identify individuals who can foster the debate, the team **selects a homogeneous group of about ten participants**. The CPE Manager handles logistics — transportation, venue, refreshments — and a **local moderator and interpreter** may be needed where the evaluators don't speak the participants' language.

<h3>Why focus groups are good for rights-holders</h3>

A focus group with rights-holders lets the team understand **first-hand the most pressing needs** of that group (and the capacity of duty-bearers to meet them). Participants can express how they benefitted from UNFPA interventions, what difference it made, and where the main gaps are.

Evaluators draw out the main issues the focus group identifies — noting where there's unanimity, and where dissenting opinions surface. Focus groups accumulate **detailed, nuanced perspectives** that other methods miss. They're also a way to scope the range of issues that may then need follow-up through select key informant interviews.

<h3>The challenges of group interviewing</h3>

<KVGrid>
  <KV title="Participant constraints">People may be influenced by relationships within the group, or by social and cultural norms — so they find it harder to express their views freely than in an individual interview.</KV>

  <KV title="Evaluator presence effects">The evaluators' own age, gender, race, language, national identity, dress, and appearance can affect participants — particularly the most vulnerable.</KV>

  <KV title="Interpersonal factors">Distance, posture, eye contact, tone of voice — all need calibration to the prevailing culture. In some settings, the gender of the evaluator should match the gender of respondents.</KV>

  <KV title="Note-taking is mandatory">Recording devices can inhibit participation. The handbook calls for another team member as note-taker, with a documentation process agreed beforehand.</KV>
</KVGrid>

<h3>Box 14 — Ethical, human-rights-responsive, gender-sensitive data collection</h3>

<Checklist storageKey="ch3-l4-ethical-collection" title="Box 14 — Ethical data collection practice">
  <Item>Evaluators are trained in collecting sensitive information; for SGBV topics they have prior experience and WHO ethical guidance familiarity.</Item>
  <Item>Data collection tools are culturally appropriate and do not create distress for respondents.</Item>
  <Item>Evaluators are aware of gender and power relations in the society; they use gender-respectful language.</Item>
  <Item>Data collection takes place at an appropriate time and place to minimise risk — including ensuring a "safe space".</Item>
  <Item>Nobody else is present within earshot except a note-taker (and where needed, a local moderator or interpreter) — with roles made clear to participants, including their confidentiality and data protection obligations.</Item>
  <Item>Each participant takes time to read and sign a consent form (or assent form where the individual cannot give legally valid consent alone).</Item>
  <Item>Evaluators understand the ethical issues and approaches for informed consent when collecting from adolescents and youth.</Item>
  <Item>Participants are informed of the topics, the data collection method, and how privacy and confidentiality will be maintained. Caution: in sensitive topics like SGBV, participants should be careful what they share in a group.</Item>
  <Item>Evaluators stress there are no "right or wrong answers" and the participants are not being evaluated. They respect the right to skip uncomfortable questions.</Item>
  <Item>Evaluators avoid highly personal, embarrassing, or threatening questions; they don't appear to endorse any one viewpoint.</Item>
  <Item>Evaluators can signpost individuals at risk to relevant support.</Item>
  <Item>Participants are informed of the CPE completion date and told the report will be available on the UNFPA public website.</Item>
</Checklist>

<InPractice country="The debrief after a focus group">
After a focus group — especially without recording — the team should run a quick debrief between the moderator, evaluator, note-taker, and interpreter (if used) to validate the transcript. Memories are freshest immediately after; what looks like consensus in the room can read differently in the notes an hour later.
</InPractice>

<KeyTerms>
  <Term name="Focus group discussion">An interactive group method (~10 participants) where the collective perspective is the unit of analysis. The moderator's job is to enable interaction.</Term>
  <Term name="Group interview">Several people interviewed together — structured more like a multi-person individual interview than a true focus group.</Term>
  <Term name="Consent form / assent form">Consent is signed by participants who can legally consent; assent is signed alongside parental/guardian consent for participants who cannot.</Term>
  <Term name="Safe space">A venue and atmosphere where the participant feels physically and psychologically able to speak — a prime consideration for sensitive topics.</Term>
</KeyTerms>
