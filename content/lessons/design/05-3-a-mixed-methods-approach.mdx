<Callout variant="key" title="Why mixed methods">
The CPE Manager must ensure the evaluation design rests solidly on a mixed methodology — collecting and combining both quantitative and qualitative data. By using different methods and sources at various points, the evaluation team **builds on the strength of each type while minimising the weaknesses of any single approach**.
</Callout>

The CO implements its interventions in a complex and dynamic economic, social, and political environment. Mixed methods help the evaluation team **increase the reliability of the data** they collect and reduce bias. Triangulation across methods, sources, and viewpoints is what gives a CPE finding its credibility.

<h3>The qualitative–quantitative balance</h3>

The CPE primarily uses **qualitative methods** for data collection — extensive document review, individual and group interviews, focus group discussions, surveys, and on-site observations during the field phase. Qualitative data must be **complemented with quantitative data** to minimise bias and strengthen the validity of findings.

Quantitative data comes from:
- Desk review of documents, websites, and online databases with financial data.
- National household surveys (DHS, MICS, censuses) providing trends on key outcome indicators.
- CO monitoring data on output indicators.
- Survey responses gathered by the evaluation team during fieldwork.

<h3>Secondary data — Table 6 advantages and limitations</h3>

Gathering secondary data through document review and analysis is the most efficient approach to gain substantial amounts of information in a short period of time.

<KVGrid>
  <KV title="Advantages">Less expensive, less time-consuming, and less resource-intensive than primary data collection. Helps avoid duplication of effort and increases the focus of primary data collection. Useful to identify existing knowledge gaps. Allows validation or complementing of primary research. Combining and synthesising multiple studies (meta-analysis) helps identify common issues and trends. Referencing authoritative sources enhances credibility.</KV>

  <KV title="Limitations">Available data may not be relevant, accurate, or up-to-date for a specific evaluation question. Data may not match the current needs or goals of the CPE. Limited scope — data may not provide enough depth or insight into important qualitative aspects. Possible biases from the source's agenda or perspective. Possible inconsistencies among data sources due to different definitions or methodologies.</KV>
</KVGrid>

<Callout variant="warn" title="A persistent gap in UNFPA monitoring data">
COARs and other UNFPA monitoring documents mostly inform **activities** — the numbers of midwives trained, upgraded health centres, equipment supplied — and largely do not adequately capture the extent to which **outputs are achieved**, and most importantly the **extent of their contribution to outcomes**. Beyond UNFPA reports, individual and group interviews, surveys, and on-site observations capture the extent of results actually achieved.
</Callout>

<h3>Primary data collection — the minimum toolkit</h3>

At the design phase, the evaluation team must design an effective package of mixed primary data collection methods. The CPE Manager must ensure that, at minimum, the data collection methods include:

<KVGrid>
  <KV title="Individual interview guides">Per cluster of key informants — see KII methodology in lesson 3.2.1.</KV>

  <KV title="Group interview guides">Group interviews and focus group discussions — see lesson 3.2.2.</KV>

  <KV title="Survey questionnaires">Where surveys are part of the design — see lesson 3.2.3.</KV>

  <KV title="On-site observation checklists">For site visits — see lesson 3.2.4.</KV>
</KVGrid>

The evaluation team must first **cluster key informants** according to their relation to the country programme and the UNFPA CO — for example: programme implementers, UN agencies and other donors, political decision-makers, rights-holders, and key players in the sector who are not involved in implementing the UNFPA programme.

<Callout variant="key" title="Evaluation questions are NOT interview questions">
Note that the evaluation questions are **not** suitable questions for interviews or surveys. They form the basis or reference framework for interview guides or survey questionnaires which, in turn, focus on core issues and themes that evaluators must address with different categories of stakeholders.

Preparing an interview guide for each cluster of key informants allows evaluators to probe for and obtain more precise and granular data during the interviews. Drafting a set of cluster-specific guides also helps the evaluation team cross-check data and follow up on points raised by interviewees.
</Callout>

<h3>Box 7 — Interview guide checklist</h3>

<Checklist storageKey="ch2-l5-interview-guides" title="Developing interview guides (Box 7)">
  <Item>**Organise stakeholders by categories** — programme implementers, donors, decision-makers, rights-holders, sectoral players outside UNFPA.</Item>
  <Item>**Identify the issues or themes** that must be raised with interviewees in each cluster, in line with the assumptions to be verified — and organise them by priority.</Item>
  <Item>**Draft questions for each cluster.**</Item>
  <Item>**Organise the questions in each cluster guide in order of priority** — most important first.</Item>
  <Item>**Ensure the number of questions is manageable** in the time available — 30–40 min for individual interviews, up to 1h30 maximum for group interviews and FGDs.</Item>
  <Item>**Ensure appropriate sequencing** of the interview (handbook references Box 12).</Item>
  <Item>**Ensure gender-sensitive and human-rights based language**, approach, and content are included (handbook references Box 14).</Item>
</Checklist>

<Callout variant="tip" title="The SWOT technique">
A technique sometimes used to generate an interview guide is the SWOT analysis — seeking views on the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats or limitations across diverse aspects of UNFPA interventions. During fieldwork, evaluators can refine and tailor the guides further as they start meeting informants.
</Callout>

<h3>Box 6 — Participation and inclusiveness</h3>

<InPractice country="Box 6 — Participation and inclusiveness">
The CPE Manager and evaluation team should **actively seek to involve the UNFPA country programme stakeholders throughout the CPE process** — preparation, design, data collection, analysis, reporting, dissemination. A participatory approach empowers participants to better analyse their own situation, which leads to better capture and interpretation of data, more reliable findings, and stronger ownership of CPE results.

In its design and implementation, the CPE should be **reflective of the principles of leaving no one behind and reaching the furthest behind**, and be inclusive of vulnerable groups and diverse voices. Particular attention should be paid to the participation of women, youth, people with disabilities, sexual and gender-based violence survivors, and key populations (including LGBTQIA+, sex workers, etc.). The CPE Manager and evaluators should consider which stakeholders should be involved — why their participation matters, at what stage of the process, and in what form — to maximise the effectiveness of a participatory and inclusive approach.
</InPractice>

<h3>What "good methodology" looks like at design</h3>

The CPE Manager must control that the evaluators have:
- Mixed the most appropriate data collection methods.
- Drafted interview guides for each stakeholder category.
- Set up themes and composition of focus groups.
- Completed survey questionnaires.
- Prepared checklists for on-site observation.

All these tools must be **annexed to the design report (annex 1)**. If the combination of tools is suboptimal at this stage, it will lead to an incomplete set of data — and organising a new collection approach during the field phase is very challenging.

<KeyTerms>
  <Term name="Triangulation">Cross-checking findings across multiple methods, sources, and viewpoints — the heart of credible mixed-methods evaluation. See lesson 3.3.</Term>
  <Term name="Secondary data">Data collected by others before the CPE — documents, reports, household surveys, partner reports.</Term>
  <Term name="Primary data">Data the evaluation team collects directly — interviews, group discussions, surveys, observations.</Term>
  <Term name="Cluster">A category of key informants — programme implementers, donors, decision-makers, rights-holders, sectoral non-UNFPA actors — used to organise interview guides.</Term>
  <Term name="LNOB">"Leave No One Behind" — the participatory inclusion principle embedded in Box 6, requiring rights-holders from marginalised groups to be visible in the methodology.</Term>
</KeyTerms>
